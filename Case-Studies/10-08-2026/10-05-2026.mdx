## Overview

This case study documents a mobile-money social engineering attack observed firsthand, involving a small business owner (referred to here as "the owner") who was targeted by an individual impersonating a Safaricom agent. Unlike most entries in this collection, this wasn't a sanctioned assessment — it's a real incident that unfolded in real time, and the response was improvised rather than procedural. It's included because the attack chain, the containment decisions made under pressure, and the aftermath all carry lessons directly relevant to how small, informal businesses in Kenya remain exposed to low-cost, high-yield fraud.

No systems were tested by the author. All technical reconstruction below is based on observed SMS notifications, direct conversation with the victim, and follow-up information from Safaricom customer care.

## The Pretext

A man carrying the tools typically used by roving Safaricom agents — SIM registration hardware, a branded shirt — approached the owner and offered to register a till number for her business. She had been using Pochi la Biashara for a long time and had no particular reason to want a till; the offer simply arrived, and she accepted it.

This detail matters: the attacker didn't need to breach anything. He supplied a plausible reason for a stranger to be standing at her counter asking for sensitive account actions, using the *appearance* of a role people are conditioned to trust.

## Credential Elicitation

Partway through the process, the owner was asked for her M-Pesa PIN. She questioned this — correctly sensing something was off — but was told it was necessary to complete the registration. Around the same time, two messages arrived that, in hindsight, were early indicators of compromise:

- An Okoa Jahazi-style loan notification for a 1024MB data bundle
- An OTP message tied to an M-Pesa till

The attacker left, claiming the registration had failed and that he'd return the next day to finish it. He never did — because he didn't need to. He had what he came for.

## What Actually Happened Technically

The initial read on the incident was that this was a SIM swap or SIM clone. It wasn't. Safaricom later confirmed the real mechanism: the attacker used the harvested phone number and PIN to log the Safaricom One (M-Pesa) app into **his own device**, establishing a live, authenticated session under the owner's identity. The physical SIM-related props were theater to justify asking for the PIN — the app itself is what needed it, not any SIM-level access.

This is a meaningfully different threat model than SIM swapping: no telco-side social engineering, no porting request, no cloned SIM. Just a stolen PIN and a second phone.

## The Fraud, in Real Time

At 00:39, a rapid sequence of transaction messages arrived:

1. An M-Shwari loan request
2. Approval of roughly 24,000 KES
3. An immediate withdrawal of the same amount to M-Pesa
4. A transfer of the funds onward to a third-party recipient

Total losses at this stage reached approximately 28,000 KES — combining that week's business earnings, the M-Shwari loan proceeds, and additional Fuliza-initiated borrowing. The speed between these steps left almost no gap for intervention; each message arrived within roughly a minute of the last.

## Containment: Lock, Unlock, Lock Again

With the fraud actively in progress, the first response was to change the M-Pesa PIN. This triggered several failed PIN attempts from the attacker's session, and the account locked.

For a few minutes, everything went quiet — long enough to wonder whether the lock had actually ended it. It hadn't. The account was briefly unlocked to check its state, and within minutes the attacker was active again. The lock, not the PIN change itself, was the only thing that had actually cut off his session.

## Racing the Attacker

Shortly after regaining access, the attacker initiated another loan and transferred it toward M-Pesa — presumably staging it for a final withdrawal to himself. This time, the response got ahead of him: the incoming funds (~15,000 KES) were redirected to a phone number the responders controlled before the attacker could complete the transfer.

That this counter-move worked at all was as much about timing as skill — a narrow window where a fast, informed response could intercept an in-flight fraudulent transaction. The account was then deliberately re-locked to end the session for good.

## The Morning After

The next day, the owner visited Safaricom customer care in town. On the way, a friend's shop confirmed the same attacker — using the same pretext — had targeted them too. This wasn't an isolated, opportunistic one-off; it was a repeatable script being run against multiple small businesses in the same area.

Safaricom confirmed the mechanism (PIN-enabled app login on the attacker's device, not a SIM-level compromise) and invalidated the attacker's session. The owner changed her business transaction number afterward, out of continued fear of being targeted again — a real operational cost layered on top of the direct financial loss.

## Reporting Friction

Recovering any of the money — or even identifying the mule accounts the funds moved through — required navigating a slow, multi-step process: obtain an Occurrence Book report from the police, bring it to Safaricom to request a permit, and only then could tracing begin. Informal payments to move the process along were, by the owner's account, effectively expected at the police stage.

This friction matters as much as the technical attack itself. Safaricom has the technical capability to trace fund flow to receiving accounts; the bottleneck is procedural and, per the owner's experience, partly extractive. For most victims — especially informal-sector business owners without the time or resources to push through that process — this alone is often where recovery efforts end.

## Lessons

- **No legitimate registration process needs your PIN.** This is the single control point that would have stopped the entire chain. Till, Pochi, and SIM registration processes do not require disclosing a personal PIN to an agent.
- **Trusted-role impersonation defeats technical awareness.** The props (shirt, hardware) did the social engineering, not any exploit. Awareness training has to account for physical pretexting, not just phishing links.
- **Account lockout is the real kill switch.** Changing the PIN wasn't enough on its own — it was the *lock state* that severed the attacker's session. Unlocking, even briefly to check status, re-opened the door.
- **Fast, informed responders can recover partial losses**, but this isn't a repeatable safety net — it required people on-site who understood the mechanics of what was happening and could act within a one-minute window.
- **Institutional recovery processes are slow and friction-heavy by design or by neglect.** The gap between "the telco can trace this" and "the victim can actually get that trace to happen" is where most of these cases die.
- **These attacks are repeated, not one-off.** The same attacker had already hit another nearby business. Small business communities sharing pretext patterns in real time (a "watch out for this guy" network) is a genuinely low-cost defense multiplier.

## Closing Note

The hardest part of this incident wasn't technical — it was watching two people come to terms with a loss that had already happened, with no clean way to undo it. The response reduced the blast radius; it didn't prevent the incident. That distinction is worth sitting with: for informal-sector business owners who don't have a security background on hand, awareness — recognizing the ask for a PIN as the red flag it is — remains the only defense that actually arrives in time.
